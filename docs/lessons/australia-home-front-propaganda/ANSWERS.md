# Teacher response guide

These are authored interpretations, not historical quotations. Accept other supported readings. Naming a technique without linking it to a visible detail is insufficient.

## Reading Source A (`read-source`)

The catalogue identifies the Department of Commerce and Agriculture, 1943, artist unknown. Visible evidence includes a smiling woman in overalls, a garden fork and wording that connects vegetables with victory. An audience of Australians able to grow food at home is a supported inference; an exclusively female audience is not established. The intended action is growing food and seeing it as a contribution to the war effort. Distinguish catalogue origin/date from observations of the poster and inferences about its audience.

## Guided interpretation (`read-guided`)

1. Identify a visible detail, such as the smile, fork or the connection between vegetables and victory.
2. A defensible completion: the poster presents gardening positively; this may encourage home food-growing by making it appear a helpful contribution. This is an intended effect, not evidence of success.
3. Many experiences are omitted: people without a garden or time/resources, people unable to do physical work, dissenting viewers and different cultural communities. A single promotional image cannot demonstrate their experiences or reactions.

## Independent Source B analysis (`read-independent`)

Department of Information, 1942, Australian War Memorial ARTV09225. The large armed soldier approaching Australia and the survival slogan support an interpretation of fear and urgency aimed at Australians who could fight or work. The image is a hostile wartime caricature. It does not prove an invasion plan or establish every viewer’s reaction. A four- or five-sentence response should connect at least two details with the maker’s intended influence.

## Source B classification (`use-source`)

1. Observation: the poster depicts a soldier approaching Australia. This describes the representation, not an actual invasion.
2. Supported inference: it aims to create urgency, supported by threat, scale and survival wording.
3. Not established: every Australian was frightened.

Its official origin and persuasive purpose make it useful for studying a government message. Lack of audience testimony limits its value for measuring reception. A source is not useless merely because it promotes a position.

## Guided usefulness (`use-guided`)

1. Useful for how food-growing was promoted: the poster explicitly connects home growing with the war effort and presents the activity positively.
2. Government origin/purpose reveals an official campaign perspective, not a neutral survey of household choices.
3. It cannot supply counts or prove that a change in growing was caused by this poster.
4. A diary could reveal one person’s response, subject to its own context and representativeness. Circulation records could show distribution but not necessarily attention; food-production or participation records could show behaviour but cannot alone isolate the poster’s causal influence. Explain why each proposed source helps and what remains uncertain.

## Independent judgement (`use-independent`)

Suggested 105-word response:

Source B is useful evidence of how the Department of Information tried to influence Australians in 1942. The armed soldier approaching Australia and the survival slogan suggest an appeal to fear and urgent action. Its government origin and persuasive purpose help explain this official message. However, it cannot show how every viewer felt or whether the poster changed behaviour. The Memorial’s later catalogue reports restricted distribution in Queensland and Melbourne; this adds context rather than universal audience testimony. Diaries could reveal individual reactions, while circulation and other contemporary records could broaden the inquiry. Several sources would be needed to assess reception across different groups.

## Comparing sources (`compare-sources`)

Similarity: both official messages link action with the war effort. Difference: A’s positive gardener and food appeal contrast with B’s hostile soldier and survival appeal. Their makers, years and contexts differ; they are not interchangeable evidence of a single audience attitude. Other sources would be needed to investigate agreement, rejection, practical barriers and experiences absent from the posters.

## Guided argument (`compare-guided`)

1. Claim connecting both strategies: propaganda promoted contribution through positive duty as well as fear.
2. A detail and reasoning: the smiling gardener/vegetables associates contribution with constructive everyday action.
3. B detail and contrast: the soldier and survival appeal makes fighting or working appear urgent.
4. Qualification: those strategies show intended persuasion, while actual response needs further evidence.

## Independent argument (`final-argument`)

Possible 169-word response:

Australian wartime propaganda tried to encourage contribution on the home front through both positive duty and fear. Source A, a 1943 Department of Commerce and Agriculture poster, connects growing vegetables with victory. Its smiling gardener and garden fork present practical work as a positive contribution. This suggests that ordinary household activity could be framed as part of the war effort. Source B, produced by the Department of Information in 1942, uses a contrasting strategy. Its large armed soldier approaching Australia and survival slogan present danger and urgent action. Fighting or working is framed as necessary for survival.

Both posters communicate official priorities, but neither represents every Australian’s perspective. Their different origins and contexts help explain their messages, while their persuasive purposes limit what they establish about public attitudes. They show how their makers sought to influence people; they do not prove that all viewers agreed or changed behaviour. Audience accounts and wider contemporary records would be needed to investigate impact, and one diary would still represent only one person’s experience.

Assess each dimension from 0–2 for formative feedback (10 possible points):

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Claim | No answer to inquiry | Relevant but general claim | Clear qualified claim relating both strategies |
| Evidence | Unsupported assertions | Specific detail from one source | Accurate details from both sources |
| Reasoning/comparison | Retrieval only | Partial explanation | Details explicitly support a meaningful similarity/difference |
| Origin/context/perspective | Misidentifies source or treats image as universal fact | Some accurate contextual awareness | Explains official perspectives and distinguishes omitted audience perspectives |
| Limits/impact | Treats intention as proof of success | States a general limitation | Explains the limit and proposes useful corroboration with its limits |
