# Australia on the Home Front: Propaganda and Persuasion during WWII

Year 10 / NSW Stage 5. A focused source inquiry, not a survey of WWII. Allow approximately two 50-minute lessons; adjust to students’ prior source-analysis experience.

Inquiry: **How did wartime propaganda try to influence Australians on the home front, and what can historical sources tell us about its purpose and impact?**

Open `../../../lessons/australia-home-front-propaganda.html` for the student lesson. Import `../../../lessons/australia-home-front-propaganda/lesson.json` into the JSON dialogue of `../../../lesson-studio.html` for editing. Study responses are on paper. No accounts or persistent response storage are required.

## Teaching sequence

1. Overview and learning intentions. Explain that intended persuasion differs from demonstrated impact.
2. Reading propaganda as evidence: introduction → concise context → Source A task → optional model → guided interpretation → independent Source B analysis.
3. Usefulness and limitations: introduction → observation/inference/corroboration → Source B classification → later catalogue context → guided Source A evaluation → independent judgement.
4. Comparing and arguing: introduction → both posters and comparison task → argument notes → guided plan → 150–200 word independent argument with both sources available → reflection and shared end page.

Three empty optional teacher-video slots exist in editable data and are omitted from the student sequence. No video playback has been claimed or tested. Source and task is the default state. Model analysis is a voluntary second state, not an automatically displayed answer. In the final argument, the second state is only a self-check. These models make this suitable for formative classroom practice; do not treat it as a locked assessment.

Students may revisit source activities from the outline during separate guided practice. On the final argument page both sources are included directly. On smaller screens the existing Evidence + task composition stacks evidence before the task; two posters remain separate evidence parts, not a fabricated Visual + companion relationship. Scrolling is necessary. Back/Next moves between activities; the two state buttons stay within the same activity.

## Sources and interpretation

- **Source A:** *Do your bit on the Food Front*, 1943, Department of Commerce and Agriculture; individual artist unknown. Australian War Memorial, ARTV02452. [Collection record](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/C98007).
- **Source B:** *He’s coming south*, 1942, Department of Information. Australian War Memorial, ARTV09225. [Collection record](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/C374232).

Both records identify the items as copyright expired/public domain. The original AWM downloadable screen images are retained unaltered in `sources/`; the supplied images are 426 × 654 and 428 × 654 pixels. These are institutional screen derivatives, not high-resolution scans. The editable JSON and independent HTML embed the same bytes. Attribution, exact URLs, maker/date, accession, rights, alt text and retrieval date are in [source-catalogue.json](source-catalogue.json). Captions expose the origin, date, institution and record locator. Exact URLs remain in the source catalogue; the player’s existing plain-text image captions do not create clickable source links.

The Food Front catalogue informs the short supply/transport context. The later Source B catalogue provides the account of limits to distribution and distinguishes wartime fears from later knowledge of Japanese strategy. These modern institutional descriptions are contextual interpretations, not contemporaneous testimony. No synthetic source or invented quotation is used. The posters themselves are authentic primary evidence of persuasive messages; their catalogue dates and makers are metadata.

Source B employs a hostile racial caricature. Discuss its construction and purpose critically: it is evidence of wartime propaganda, not a description of Japanese people. Do not present the poster as proof that invasion was inevitable, that all Australians feared invasion, or that the campaign worked. Source A’s positive image likewise does not establish that all women gardened or that only women were targeted.

## Learning evidence

Use [ANSWERS.md](ANSWERS.md) as a guide to defensible interpretations. Accept other interpretations supported by the source. An unsupported assertion of universal audience response is a misconception, not an alternative evidence-based interpretation. For the final argument assess claim, evidence from both sources, explanatory comparison, attention to origin/context and a qualified judgement about impact. The end page does not certify mastery.

For support, ask students to point to a detail before writing an inference; give the guided sentence stem. For extension, ask what a diary, circulation record or food-production series could and could not establish. The lesson does not contain those additional sources and does not pretend to have corroborated reception.
