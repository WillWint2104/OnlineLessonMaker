Where does y = x2 − 4 cross the x-axis, and why there?

1 Algebra2 Visual check3 Conclusion

State 1 — Algebra

Finding the roots

Question

Find the values of x for which y = x2 − 4 is zero.

Worked solution

  1. A crossing of the x-axis is a point where y = 0.
    x2 − 4 = 0
  2. Rearrange.
    x2 = 4
  3. Take the root of both sides, carrying both signs.
    x = ±2
Answerx = −2 and x = 2

Why it agrees

The whole curve y = x2 has been moved down by 4, so the vertex now sits at (0, −4) instead of the origin.

That drop is what creates the two crossings: the curve dips below the axis and must come back up through it on each side. The crossings are at x = −2 and x = 2, matching the algebra.

The distance from the axis of symmetry to each crossing is the square root of how far the vertex was moved down — here √4 = 2. Move the curve down by 9 instead and the crossings would be at ±3.

So the constant does two jobs at once: it sets the vertex's height and, through that, the spacing of the roots. Reading the graph makes the two look like one fact rather than two rules.

State 3 — Conclusion

What this shows

Subtracting a constant lowers the whole parabola. Whether it crosses the x-axis twice, once or not at all is decided by how far the vertex sits below or above the axis — so the number of solutions to x2 − c = 0 is a question about the vertex, not about the algebra.
