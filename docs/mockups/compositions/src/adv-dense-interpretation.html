Why it agrees

Solving x2 = 16 asks where the curve y = x2 reaches a height of 16.

x = ±4

Draw the horizontal line y = 16. It crosses the curve twice, at x = −4 and x = 4 — the two values the algebra produced.

The two crossings sit at equal distances either side of the y-axis. That is the graph saying the same thing as the ± in the working: squaring destroys the sign, so the two solutions are a mirror pair rather than two unrelated numbers.

The height of the line is what decides how many solutions there are. A line above the vertex crosses twice. A line through the vertex touches once, which is the case x2 = 0. A line below the vertex never meets the curve at all, which is what a negative right-hand side would mean — and is why x2 = −16 has no real solution.

Reading the same question two ways is the point of the check: the algebra gives the values, and the graph says why there are exactly that many of them.

It also shows what would change if the question did. Ask for x2 = 4 and the line drops; the crossings slide inwards to ±2. Ask for x2 = 25 and they slide out to ±5. The shape never changes — only the height at which it is cut.
