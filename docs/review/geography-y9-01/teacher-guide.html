Inquiry: How is Australia’s population distributed, and what might explain one pattern?

Approximately 60 minutes. Use an exercise book, pencil and calculator if needed. Students use the website to read evidence and use the existing source inspector; they do not complete an untested typed workbook. Timing is a planning estimate, not classroom validation.

Core and extension workload

Allow eight minutes for the independent response: two to select evidence and plan, five to write, one to check. The core is 60–90 words, about four or five sentences, with one qualified mechanism. An optional extension develops a second mechanism towards 100–140 words. The guided activity is a three-minute four-point plan, not another full paragraph. All essential concepts, sources, six tasks and the ten-activity journey are retained.

Sources A and B establish population evidence. They do not show the observed distribution of employment, services or infrastructure. Those remain explanations to test with additional evidence.

Source and conceptual cautions

  • Read the dated ABS 2023–24 release consistently: June 2024 preliminary ERP, not current figures. Table B uses four selected GCCSAs, not urban/rural population totals.
  • Map A is genuine ABS modelled density evidence with an explicitly adapted plate. Ask for regional observations, not exact cell values from a reduced JPEG. Use Inspect source and its task/provenance drawer.
  • Do not confuse the first illustrative share-change diagram (40% to 50%) with the next hypothetical equal-growth calculation (40 of 100 to 48 of 120, still 40%).
  • Distinguish population distribution, settlement arrangement, density, counts, growth rates and urban share. A stock map cannot establish change, migration motives or a causal explanation.
  • Grey cells do not mean empty or unoccupied Country. Avoid erasing Aboriginal communities, ongoing connection to Country or the significance of low-density areas.
  • Students may reasonably suggest access, environmental or historical mechanisms, but must explain them as hypotheses requiring appropriate evidence.

Models and feedback for all six tasks

y9-01-count-share

Task: In the illustrative place, 40 urban and 60 rural residents each increase by 20%.

  1. Calculate each new count and the new total.
  2. Calculate the new urban share. Is this urban population growth, urbanisation, both or neither? Explain.

Answer/criteria: 48 urban; 72 rural; 120 total; urban share 40%. Urban population growth without urbanisation.

Reasoning/model: Forty multiplied by 1.2 is 48; sixty multiplied by 1.2 is 72. Divide 48 by 120 and multiply by 100: 40%. More urban residents does not by itself show a rising urban share.

y9-01-map

Task: Use Source A in Inspect source, then answer in your exercise book.

  1. Identify the producer, population reference date, unit and what each coloured grid cell represents.
  2. Describe two located concentrations and contrast them with a broad interior area. Name regions, not exact streets.
  3. Identify an exception to “everyone lives on the east coast”. Use the map, not a guess.
  4. Explain why a grey cell and a national-scale thumbnail do not prove that a place has no people, communities, activity or significance.

Answer/criteria: ABS, June 2024; modelled people per 1 km². Concentrations along eastern/south-eastern Australia and a separate south-western concentration contrast with much of the interior. The south-west is an exception to the east-coast-only claim. Modelled zero is not proof of absence.

Reasoning/model: A strong description locates the eastern/south-eastern concentrations and contrasts them with extensive grey interior cells. The south-western concentration is an explicit exception. Smaller inland and northern patches also prevent a coast-only absolute claim. The grid models resident population from SA1 estimates and residential address points; modelling, reduction and the time reference limit fine-scale conclusions. Do not interpret grey as unoccupied Country or absence of Aboriginal communities.

y9-01-table

Task: Compare Sydney and Brisbane using Source B.

  1. Which had more residents in June 2024? By how many?
  2. Which added more residents in 2023–24? By how many? Which had the higher percentage growth?
  3. Why can you not use these four rows to state Australia’s urban share or rank these regions by density?

Answer/criteria: Sydney had 2,777,170 more residents and added 34,608 more residents than Brisbane. Brisbane’s growth rate was higher: 2.7% compared with 2.0%. Urban share and density cannot be obtained from these rows alone.

Reasoning/model: Subtract 2,780,063 from 5,557,233 to obtain 2,777,170 residents. Subtract 72,930 from 107,538 to obtain 34,608 added residents. Compare the published rates separately. The table lacks the whole national urban population and total population under a consistent urban classification; it also lacks land areas. GCCSAs include non-urban land and are not a national urban/rural classification.

y9-01-guided

Task: Explain a concentration shown on Source A.

  1. Observation: locate one concentration and contrast it with another region.
  2. Evidence: identify the source date, what the colour represents and a relevant numerical comparison from B. Do not treat city counts as density.
  3. Inference: propose one possible influence and explain the mechanism.
  4. Qualification: name a missing source that could test the inference and explain one limit of A or B.

Answer/criteria: A complete response combines a located map observation, accurately labelled evidence, a possible mechanism and a test/limitation.

Reasoning/model: Source A’s June 2024 grid shows concentrated populated cells along eastern and south-eastern Australia compared with much of the interior. Source B separately records 5,557,233 residents in Greater Sydney and 2,780,063 in Greater Brisbane; those are city-region counts, not density values. Access to employment and services may help concentrate settlement because people can seek work and services near established centres. A dated employment-and-transport map would help test that mechanism; neither A nor B directly measures motives or proves the cause.

y9-01-independent

Task: How is Australia’s population distributed, and what might explain one pattern? Use Sources A and B.

  1. Describe a located distribution pattern from A and one exception.
  2. Support your response with one accurate comparison from B, stating date/period and units.
  3. Explain one possible influence and how it might affect the pattern. Employment, services and infrastructure are hypotheses here, not activities directly measured by A or B.
  4. State a source limitation and propose specific additional evidence to test one explanation.

Answer/criteria: Core criteria: a located pattern and exception; an accurate labelled comparison; one qualified mechanism; a specific source limitation and proposed test. Extension: develop a second mechanism. The original longer model remains an extension example in the teacher guide.

Core model (60–90 words): Source A’s June 2024 grid shows concentrations in eastern and south-eastern Australia and a separate south-western concentration. Source B records 5,557,233 residents in Greater Sydney, more than Greater Brisbane’s 2,780,063 at June 2024; these are counts, not densities. Access to jobs may attract residents to centres by reducing the distance between home and work. That is a hypothesis, not an activity pattern measured by A or B. A dated employment map could test it; the modelled population grid cannot show people’s motives.

Preserved extension model (100–140 words; optional): Australia’s June 2024 modelled grid shows concentrations along eastern and south-eastern Australia compared with much of the interior; the south-western concentration is an exception to an east-coast-only description. In the ABS 2023–24 release, Greater Sydney had 5,557,233 residents and Greater Brisbane 2,780,063 at June 2024: these are city-region counts, not densities. Employment and service access may attract residents towards established centres. Earlier infrastructure investment may reinforce that clustering by improving access. These mechanisms are inferences: the map does not record people’s reasons for settling. Comparing dated employment and transport evidence with the grid could test the access explanation. Modelled 1 km² cells and national-scale reduction also limit conclusions about individual homes or communities.

y9-01-reflect

Task: Correct the claims and revise your explanation.

  1. “More city residents always means urbanisation.” Use the illustrative 40/60 example to correct this.
  2. “A red density cell means the fastest population growth.” Identify the missing evidence.
  3. “The map proves everyone moves to the coast for jobs.” Separate the overstatement, observation and untested cause.
  4. Revise one sentence in your independent response to make its evidence or uncertainty more precise. Record one question that A and B cannot answer.

Answer/criteria: Urban growth can occur at an unchanged urban share; density is not change; neither a universal coast-only pattern nor a migration motive is proved. Revision should improve source use or qualification.

Reasoning/model: The illustrative population grows from 40 urban of 100 to 48 of 120: still 40% urban, so growth without urbanisation. A red cell is a high modelled density for June 2024; comparable populations at two dates would be needed for growth. A shows concentrations plus exceptions, not everyone’s movement or motives. A useful revision names a source/date/unit, replaces “proves” with a qualified inference, or adds the evidence needed to test it.

Independent explanation: analytic criteria (formative)

CriterionSecureFeedback if developing
Located pattern and exceptionDescribes a located population concentration and identifies a map-supported exception to an east-coast-only pattern, such as the south-western concentration.Ask “where?”; replace absolutes such as everyone, nowhere or empty.
Accurate evidenceUses A plus a correct B comparison with reference period and units; does not call a city-region count a density.Circle the denominator/unit and identify whether this is a count, change, rate or density.
ExplanationCore: one possible influence has an explained mechanism and qualified language. Extension: develop a second. Observations and inferences remain distinct.Ask “how might that influence the pattern?” rather than accepting a list of factors.

No automatic marking or fixed summative weight. Accept other supported regional observations and well-reasoned hypotheses. A model paragraph is an example, not a script to memorise.

Support and extension

Support: allow short sentence stems or an oral rehearsal; assess the four criteria rather than penalising a student who needs adjusted writing support. Read the legend aloud; provide sentence stems “Source A shows… whereas…” and “This may reflect… because…; to test this…”. Let students explain orally before writing. Extension: explain why the four GCCSA populations cannot determine urbanisation or a density ranking, and propose compatible data needed.
